===============================================================================
1. WHAT THE APP IS FOR
===============================================================================

Anyone can open a photo in an editor and change the date, move the location,
or rub out something they do not want people to see. Once that is done, the
photo looks completely normal. You cannot tell by looking.

GeoGuard fixes this. When you take a photo with GeoGuard, the app does two
things at once:

   (a) It WRITES the place, the GPS numbers and the time onto the picture,
       so a human can read it.

   (b) It LOCKS the photo with a digital signature, so a computer can later
       prove whether even one small thing was changed.

The important word is "prove". Not "guess". If somebody edits the photo, the
maths stops working, and the app says so.


===============================================================================
2. TAKING A PHOTO, STEP BY STEP
===============================================================================

When you press the shutter, this happens in order:

  STEP 1 - The app checks the GPS is good.
           If there is no GPS, the app refuses.
           If the GPS reading is older than 30 seconds, the app refuses.
           If another app is FAKING your location (some apps can do this),
           the app refuses.

           Why refuse instead of just warning? Because a photo that lies
           about where it was taken is worse than no photo at all. If we
           signed it, we would be putting our name on a lie.

  STEP 2 - The camera takes the picture.

  STEP 3 - The picture is sent to a "background worker".
           This is like handing the job to a second person so the screen
           does not freeze while the work is done.

  STEP 4 - The stamp is painted onto the picture (see part 3).

  STEP 5 - Fingerprints are taken of the picture (see part 5).

  STEP 6 - A small block of information is built, and signed with a secret
           key that never leaves your phone (see parts 4 and 6).

  STEP 7 - That block is hidden inside the photo file, in three different
           places (see part 7).

  STEP 8 - The photo is saved on the phone and copied to the gallery.

All of steps 4 to 7 happen in one pass. The picture is opened once and saved
once. This matters, because opening and saving a big photo is slow, and doing
it twice used to make the app freeze for several seconds.


===============================================================================
3. THE STAMP YOU CAN SEE
===============================================================================

The stamp is the strip of writing at the bottom of the photo. It has three
lines:

      TILAKWADI, BELAGAVI, KARNATAKA, INDIA
      Lat 15.849700 deg   Long 74.497700 deg
      23SEP26 14:30 UTC+05:30

This strip takes up the bottom 18% of the picture.

Some details that matter:

  - The writing is NOT a sticker placed on top. It is painted into the
    actual pixels of the photo. You cannot peel it off.

  - The text size is a PERCENTAGE of the photo height (about 2.2%), not a
    fixed size. If it were fixed, the writing would look tiny on a big photo
    and huge on a small one.

  - There is a solid light panel behind the words. We tried just a shadow
    first, but words with only a shadow become unreadable against a bright
    sky or a dark room. A solid panel always works.

  - The place name is looked up BEFORE you press the shutter, not after.
    Looking up a place name needs the internet, and waiting for the internet
    after pressing the shutter made the app feel broken.


===============================================================================
4. THE HIDDEN DATA INSIDE THE PHOTO
===============================================================================

Besides the writing you can see, the app hides a small block of text inside
the photo file. We call it the "envelope". It is written in JSON, which is
just text with labels.

An envelope looks roughly like this:

   {
     "lat" : 15.8497,          <- latitude
     "lon" : 74.4977,          <- longitude
     "alt" : 751.3,            <- height above sea level, in metres
     "ts"  : 1774269000000,    <- exact time, in milliseconds
     "dev" : "a91f...",        <- a code for the phone that took it
     "ph"  : "3f8a91c2...",    <- fingerprint of the stamp strip
     "st"  : [16 fingerprints] <- fingerprints of the picture itself
     "pk"  : "AqW3...",        <- the public key (explained in part 6)
     "sig" : "8b2c...|91af...",<- the signature
     "v"   : 6                 <- version number of this format
   }

About "dev": this is NOT your phone number or your IMEI. Normal apps are not
allowed to read the IMEI on modern Android, and never on iPhone. Instead we
take the identifiers the phone does allow, mix in the brand and model, and
run the result through SHA-256. The output is a 64-character code. It is the
same for every photo from that phone, but it cannot be turned back into
anything personal.


===============================================================================
5. THE FINGERPRINTS (HOW WE CATCH EDITS)
===============================================================================

A fingerprint here is a short code made from a picture. Change the picture,
and the code changes.

We do NOT use a normal file hash (like the SHA-256 of the whole file). Here
is why. A normal hash changes completely if even one bit changes. But a photo
changes slightly just from being saved and re-opened, without anybody editing
it. A normal hash would call every honest photo a fake.

So we use something called a dHash, which is a "looks-like" fingerprint.

HOW A dHASH IS MADE:

   1. Shrink the picture down to 9 pixels wide and 8 pixels tall.
   2. For each row, compare every pixel with the one to its right.
   3. If the left one is brighter, write down 1. Otherwise write down 0.
   4. You now have 8 rows x 8 comparisons = 64 ones and zeros.

That is the fingerprint. It records whether things get brighter or darker
going across the image. Saving the photo again barely changes that, but a
real edit changes it a lot.

TWO SETS OF FINGERPRINTS:

   (a) The stamp strip gets ONE fingerprint of its own.
       This catches anybody who edits the GPS numbers or the time.

   (b) The picture above the strip is cut into a 4 x 4 grid = 16 squares,
       and EACH square gets its own fingerprint.

Why 16 separate fingerprints instead of one for the whole picture? Because
one fingerprint for the whole photo is too blunt. If you erase one person
from the corner of a large photo, a single whole-photo fingerprint hardly
moves at all. But the fingerprint of THAT ONE SQUARE moves a lot. Splitting
the picture is what makes small edits visible.

HOW MUCH CHANGE IS ALLOWED:

We compare two fingerprints by counting how many of the 64 bits are
different. This count is called the Hamming distance.

   0 to 10 different  -> fine, this is normal saving and re-opening
   11 or more         -> something was edited

The app shows this to you as "3 OF 64 SPOTS DIFFER - LIMIT 10".

6. THE KEYS AND THE SIGNATURE
===============================================================================

This is the heart of the app, so it is worth reading slowly.

WHAT A SIGNATURE IS

A digital signature is a number that can only be produced by someone holding
a particular secret. Anybody can check the number is correct, but nobody can
produce it without the secret.

TWO KEYS, NOT ONE

When you first open GeoGuard, it creates a pair of keys using a standard
called ECDSA P-256:

   PRIVATE KEY  - stays locked inside your phone forever. Never sent
                  anywhere. Never shown. Used to SIGN.

   PUBLIC KEY   - a short code, 33 bytes long. It is placed INSIDE every
                  photo you take. Used to CHECK.

The two are mathematically linked. The public key can check a signature. It
CANNOT create one. So it is safe for it to travel inside the photo where
anyone can read it.

WHAT GETS SIGNED

The app builds one long line of text containing everything that matters:

   v6|latitude|longitude|altitude|time|phonecode|stampprint|16 prints|publickey

Then it signs that whole line. Two things follow from this:

   - If anybody changes ANY of those values, the line changes, and the
     signature no longer matches.

   - The public key is inside the signed line. So an attacker cannot swap in
     their own public key and claim they took your photo. Swapping the key
     changes the line, which breaks the signature.

THE SIGNATURE ITSELF

It comes out as two numbers, written as "r|s" in hexadecimal. The app uses a
deterministic method (RFC 6979), which means it does not need a source of
randomness at the moment of signing. That is what allows signing to happen
in the background worker.

WHY WE CHANGED THIS (IMPORTANT)

The first version of the app used HMAC-SHA256 instead. HMAC uses ONE shared
secret for both signing and checking.

That created a serious problem. Only the phone holding the secret could check
the photo. So if you sent your photo to a friend, their app looked for a key
that matched, found nothing, and said the photo was FAKE. It was not fake.
Their app simply had no way to check.

You also cannot fix this by sharing the secret, because anyone holding it
could forge photos.

Moving to a key PAIR fixed it completely. Now the public key rides along
inside the photo, so any phone on earth can check any GeoGuard photo, with no
internet and no account.


===============================================================================
7. WHERE THE HIDDEN DATA IS STORED (THREE COPIES)
===============================================================================

The envelope is written into the photo file in three different places:

   COPY 1 - EXIF "UserComment"
            EXIF is the standard area where cameras store settings, date and
            GPS. Most respectful tools keep it.

   COPY 2 - A JPEG COM segment
            JPEG files officially allow a "comment" block. We insert a real
            one right after the file header.

   COPY 3 - At the very end of the file
            After the JPEG officially ends, we simply add our text. Image
            viewers ignore anything after the end marker, so the photo still
            opens normally everywhere.

Each copy starts with a marker so we can find it again:
   "VPIC_METADATA:" for copies 1 and 2, and "VPIC_PAYLOAD:" for copy 3.

Why three copies? Because different programs destroy different parts. Some
strip EXIF for privacy. Some rewrite the file and lose the comment. Some cut
off anything after the end. With three copies in different places, the
envelope usually survives at least one of them.

When checking, the app looks for the end-of-file copy first, because it is
the toughest, then falls back to the others.


===============================================================================
8. CHECKING A PHOTO
===============================================================================

When you pick a photo and ask the app to check it, four checks run in order.
Each one is shown on screen as it happens.

   CHECK 1 - LOOKING FOR THE HIDDEN DETAILS
             Search the file for the envelope. If nothing is found, the
             photo was either not taken with GeoGuard, or its details were
             stripped out by something along the way. Stop here.

   CHECK 2 - CHECKING THE SIGNATURE
             Rebuild the signed line from the envelope. Use the public key
             inside the photo to check the signature. If it does not match,
             something in the details was changed. Stop here.

   CHECK 3 - CHECKING THE STAMP
             Take a fresh fingerprint of the stamp strip in the photo we are
             holding now. Compare it with the one recorded in the envelope.
             If more than 10 of the 64 bits differ, the GPS text or the time
             was edited. Stop here.

   CHECK 4 - CHECKING THE PICTURE
             Take 16 fresh fingerprints, one per square. Compare each with
             the recorded one. If any square is over the limit, that part of
             the picture was edited.

If all four pass, the verdict is "Real photo".

Notice the order. We check the signature BEFORE looking at pixels. There is
no point comparing fingerprints if the recorded fingerprints themselves
could have been swapped. The signature is what makes the recorded values
trustworthy in the first place.

Every failure names WHAT went wrong: "Stamp edited", "Photo edited",
"Details changed", "Not taken with GeoGuard".


===============================================================================
9. KNOWING WHO TOOK THE PHOTO
===============================================================================

Here is something that is easy to get wrong, so the app keeps two questions
completely separate:

   QUESTION A: "Has this photo been changed since it was signed?"
               Pure maths. The answer is the same on every phone in the
               world. The signature answers this.

   QUESTION B: "Who took it?"
               The signature does NOT answer this. Anybody can create their
               own key pair. A valid signature only proves that whoever
               holds that key signed this photo. It does not say who they
               are.

So the app shows four levels:

   "Taken on this phone"            - the key is one of mine
   "Taken by Ravi"                  - I have saved this key under that name
   "Taken on another GeoGuard phone"- valid, but I have never seen this key
   "Not real"                       - the signature failed

HOW A KEY GETS A NAME

Each user has a short code made from their public key, shown in groups of
four like this:

      5DE6 6BAF 27E8 25AA

When a friend sends you a photo and you check it, the app offers "Save this
sender". You give them a name. From then on, every photo from them says
"Taken by Ravi". This is called trust on first use, and it is the same idea
SSH uses when it asks "are you sure you want to connect?".

You can also read the code out loud to your friend over a phone call to make
sure it matches what their app shows. Two people comparing codes directly is
the strongest check there is, because nothing in the middle can lie about it.

10. SENDING A PHOTO SAFELY
===============================================================================

THE PROBLEM

If you send a GeoGuard photo through WhatsApp as a normal photo, WhatsApp
squeezes it to make it smaller. Squeezing means rebuilding the file. That
destroys all three copies of the envelope. The photo arrives looking fine,
but all the proof is gone.

So there are two ways to send a photo:

WAY 1 - SHARE AS A FILE (free, works with no account)

Use the "Share this photo" button and send it as a FILE or DOCUMENT, not as
a photo. The file arrives untouched and everything still works. The app says
this on screen.

WAY 2 - SEND INSIDE THE APP (locked, end to end)

This is the safer way, and it borrows the idea WhatsApp uses for its own
encryption.

   1. The app makes a fresh random key, 32 bytes long, just for this one
      photo.

   2. The photo file is locked with that key using AES-256-GCM. GCM is
      important: it does not only hide the contents, it also detects if even
      one byte was changed afterwards.

   3. Now the key itself has to reach your friend. The app makes a
      throwaway key pair, combines it with your friend's public key using
      ECDH, and passes the result through HKDF. Both sides can work out the
      same shared value, but nobody watching can.

      (In plain terms: two people can agree on a secret in public, without
      ever saying the secret out loud.)

   4. The locked photo is uploaded to the server. The server also records
      the SHA-256 of the locked file.

   5. Your friend's app downloads it, checks the SHA-256 first, works out
      the same shared value, unlocks the key, unlocks the photo, and saves
      it.

THE POINT

The server stores a meaningless block of scrambled bytes. It cannot see the
photo, the place, or the time. And because it cannot READ the photo, it
cannot RE-SAVE it either. So the bytes that arrive are exactly the bytes
that were signed, and the signature still works.

The encryption is not only for privacy here. It is what keeps the proof
alive.

Two smaller details:
   - The hash is checked BEFORE unlocking. That way a broken download is
     reported as a broken download, not as a fake photo.
   - Two copies of the same photo produce completely different scrambled
     files, so nobody can tell from the server that you sent the same photo
     twice.

11. LOGIN WITH GOOGLE
===============================================================================

Login exists for one reason: so friends can find each other by a username
instead of reading out long codes.

HOW IT WORKS

   1. You tap "Sign in with Google". Google shows its own screen.

   2. Google gives the app a signed ticket called an ID token.

   3. The app sends that ticket to OUR server.

   4. Our server downloads Google's public keys and checks the ticket really
      came from Google, and that it was made for OUR app and not some other
      app.

   5. Only then does the server create your account and give the app a
      session pass.

THE RULE THAT MATTERS

The app never tells the server who you are. It shows a ticket signed by
Google, and the server checks that signature itself. If the server simply
believed what the app said, anybody could edit the app and log in as anyone.

WHY THE DATABASE IS NOT INSIDE THE APP

An Android APK file is just a zip. Anyone can unzip it and read every piece
of text inside it in about one minute. If the database password were in the
app, it would be public, and anyone could read or delete everything.

So the app never touches the database. It talks to our server over HTTPS,
and only the server holds the password.

     App  ---HTTPS--->  Our server  --->  MongoDB
                        (holds password)

12. PAYMENTS
===============================================================================

Three things cost money, each Rs 1 for testing:

   - Checking whether a photo is real
   - Sending a photo to another user
   - Exporting the PDF evidence report

Taking photos and sharing as a file stay free.

HOW A PAYMENT IS PROVED

   1. The app asks our server to start an order.
   2. The server tells Razorpay the amount. THE APP NEVER SENDS THE AMOUNT.
      The price list lives on the server, so a modified app cannot buy a
      Rs 1 item for 1 paisa.
   3. The payment screen opens. You pay.
   4. Razorpay gives back a payment id and a signature.
   5. The app passes these to our server.
   6. The server recreates the signature itself using the secret key that
      only it holds. If they match, the feature unlocks. If not, nothing
      happens.

Step 6 is the whole point. The app never decides that it paid. If it did,
somebody would edit the app to always say "paid" within a week.

Two more safeguards:
   - The comparison is done in constant time. A normal comparison finishes
     early on the first wrong character, and the tiny timing difference can
     be measured and used to guess the signature one character at a time.
   - There is a webhook. If you pay and then close the app before it comes
     back, Razorpay tells our server directly, and the feature still unlocks.

13. WHAT THE DATABASE STORES
===============================================================================

MongoDB holds five things. Note that PHOTOS ARE NEVER STORED AS PICTURES in
the database, only as locked scrambled blocks, and only until collected.

   users
      Google account id, email, name, username, public key, sharing code,
      what they have paid for, when they were last seen, and counters
      (how many logins, photos sent, photos received, checks run).

   shares
      Who sent to whom, where the locked block is, its hash, and the wrapped
      key. Deleted as soon as the receiver opens it.

   shareBlobs
      The locked scrambled photo files themselves.

   payments
      Every order: which plan, how much, whether it was paid, and whether a
      signature was ever rejected.

   events
      A diary of everything that happens: signed up, signed in, username
      taken, key published, order started, payment accepted, payment
      rejected, photo sent, photo opened, photo checked.

WHY THE DIARY EXISTS

Without it, the database only tells you what is true RIGHT NOW. The first
time a user says "I paid and got nothing", a right-now-only database cannot
answer them. The diary can.

WHAT IS DELIBERATELY NOT STORED

When the app reports that a check happened, it sends the VERDICT ONLY. Not
the photo, not the coordinates, not the signature. The server cannot read a
locked photo, so it should not be able to learn those things from the diary
either. There is an automatic test that proves the app cannot sneak extra
fields in.

The diary also deletes itself after 90 days, which is long enough to settle
a payment argument and short enough to keep the free 512 MB database from
filling up.

14. WHERE EACH PART LIVES IN THE CODE
===============================================================================

THE APP (folder: flutter_app/lib)

  services/camera_service.dart    Taking the photo, running the background
                                  worker, saving the file.
  services/overlay_service.dart   Painting the stamp onto the pixels.
  services/security_service.dart  The envelope, the fingerprints, hiding the
                                  data in the file, and checking it.
  services/identity_service.dart  The key pairs, signing, saved contacts.
  services/sealed_transfer.dart   Locking and unlocking a photo for sending.
  services/verification_service.dart  The four checks, in order.
  services/account_service.dart   Google login, the username directory,
                                  sending and receiving.
  services/payment_service.dart   The payment screen.
  services/frame_store.dart       Keeping track of saved photos.
  services/certificate_service.dart  The PDF report.

  screens/camera_screen.dart      The camera screen.
  screens/verify_screen.dart      The checking screen.
  screens/senders_screen.dart     Your code and your saved senders.
  screens/inbox_screen.dart       Photos people sent you.
  screens/paywall.dart            One place that decides if you have paid.

THE SERVER (folder: server/src)

  index.js     Starts everything up.
  app.js       All the web addresses (routes).
  auth.js      Checking the Google ticket, making session passes.
  db.js        Connecting to MongoDB. The ONLY file that reads the password.
  keys.js      Checking a public key really is a valid key.
  shares.js    Storing and handing out locked photos.
  payments.js  Razorpay orders and signature checking.
  events.js    The diary.

15. THE BIG PROBLEMS WE FIXED
===============================================================================

  PROBLEM: A photo sent to a friend was always reported as fake.
  CAUSE:   HMAC uses one shared secret, so only the phone that made the
           photo could check it.
  FIX:     Switched to a key pair. The public half now travels inside the
           photo, so any phone can check any photo.

  PROBLEM: Pressing the shutter took several seconds and froze the screen.
  CAUSE:   After pressing, the app asked for a brand new GPS reading, then
           looked up the place name over the internet, then opened and saved
           the full-size photo TWICE, all on the screen's own thread.
  FIX:     Reuse the GPS reading and place name already on screen, and do
           one open and one save inside a background worker.

  PROBLEM: A new photo did not appear until the app was closed and reopened.
  CAUSE:   The photo list was read once when the app started and never
           again. Also, photos were being saved to the temporary folder,
           which Android empties whenever it needs space.
  FIX:     The list now reloads when a photo is taken, when you switch tabs,
           and when you return to the app. Photos moved to permanent
           storage.

  PROBLEM: Capture sometimes failed with a strange error.
  CAUSE:   The background worker was accidentally trying to carry the whole
           camera object with it, which is not allowed.
  FIX:     Only plain data is handed to the worker now, and if the worker
           cannot start at all, the job runs normally instead of failing.

  PROBLEM: The words in the app were too technical. Things like "Extracting
           EXIF / COM payload" and "HAMMING 3 / 64".
  FIX:     Rewritten in plain words: "Looking for the hidden details",
           "3 OF 64 SPOTS DIFFER - LIMIT 10".


===============================================================================
IN ONE PARAGRAPH
===============================================================================

GeoGuard writes the place and time onto a photo where you can see it, takes
64-bit "looks-like" fingerprints of the stamp and of sixteen separate parts
of the picture, and signs all of it with a private key that never leaves the
phone. The matching public key is hidden inside the photo in three different
places, so any other phone can check the signature offline and find out
whether anything was changed. Photos can be sent locked so that not even our
own server can read them, which also means nothing can re-save them and
destroy the proof. Google login and usernames make friends easy to find, and
payments are always confirmed by the server, never by the app.
